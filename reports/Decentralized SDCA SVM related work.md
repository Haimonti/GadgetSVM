# No paper yet gossips additive SVM duals

**Bottom line: none of the five sets of research notes found a published paper that does either of your methods. The threat to the two methods is different, though.** The linear method (local SDCA, random k-out gossip, versioned per-origin A_k·Δα_k contributions that receivers add) has no direct prior art. Its nearest neighbours are COLA, which averages primal vectors with a mixing matrix instead of adding dual contributions, and the Cho/Lai/Xu tree and ring dual coordinate ascent papers, which are synchronous and use a fixed topology. The nonlinear method has older prior art for its basic idea. Sending support vectors around a network to train a decentralized SVM goes back to 2006–2012 (Lu et al. DPSVM, Xu et al. FDSVM, and the Flouri et al. gossip SVMs). You therefore cannot claim "first decentralized SVM that exchanges support vectors". You can claim the combination of an SDCA solver, signed duals that receivers add without re-solving, and support vectors compressed by a shared-seed projection before the RBF kernel. For baselines, the best new additions beyond what you already have are: for the linear method, **P2Pegasos / gossip learning (Ormándi, Hegedűs, Jelasity)**, **Lan & Zhou's asynchronous primal-dual method**, and **COLA** if you have not run it yet; for the nonlinear method, **COKE/DKLA** and **an FDSVM/DPSVM-style support-vector flooding baseline**. One warning about the evidence: the notes established most of this from abstracts and snippets. The papers that matter most for the nonlinear novelty claim, the support-vector-exchange papers, were not read in full.

## The linear method's nearest rivals average where you add

The notes split the decentralized dual literature into two families, and the split protects the linear method. The first family uses the **consensus dual**: one dual variable per network edge, enforcing "my w equals your w". In these papers a gossip step turns out to be a coordinate step on that dual. Examples are ESDACD (AISTATS 2019) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02660)), Setwise Coordinate Descent (IEEE TAC 2025) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19004)), Notarnicola's randomized dual proximal gradient ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05713)) and StochaLM ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11432)). Almost all of them **require smooth, strongly convex losses**, so the hinge loss is outside their guarantees. The second family uses the **data dual** you use, with one α per training example and w built from those α's. Here the only decentralized member is COLA ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.04883)). Everything else in this family has a server or a cluster: Hybrid-DCA, Dis-dfSDCA, Nathan & Klabjan, and FedDCD ([arXiv 1610.10060](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.10060)). arXiv searches for "SDCA AND decentralized", "SDCA AND graph" and "CoCoA AND decentralized" **returned zero abstracts** ([arXiv API](https://export.arxiv.org/api/query?search_query=abs:SDCA+AND+abs:decentralized)).

A citation chase over the 144 Semantic Scholar citers of COLA and 179 citers of CoCoA+ found no gossip, asynchronous or additive extension of either ([S2 COLA citations](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/arXiv:1808.04883/citations)). What it found instead is the Cho/Lai/Xu line, which runs CoCoA-style dual coordinate ascent over a **tree** (JSAC 2021, ICASSP 2019) and a **ring** (Asilomar 2025). These are synchronous papers with fixed topologies and no random gossip ([arXiv 1703.04785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04785)). You already know the tree paper. The **Asilomar 2025 ring paper is new and should be cited**. It is the closest "CoCoA on a peer topology" work and was published recently. Newer work on decentralized SVMs has moved to primal methods on a smoothed hinge. Examples are Gbs-SVM, a 2026 SSRN preprint using gossip with gradient tracking ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6270798)), and the convoluted-SVM decentralized ADMM of 2025 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07563)).

## The linear method's novelty survives, framed as adding over gossip

**Closest prior art: COLA**, followed by the Cho/Lai/Xu tree and ring dual coordinate ascent. You can defend four differences. First, COLA mixes primal vectors with a doubly-stochastic matrix whose step size depends on the network's spectral gap. Your receivers instead **add versioned per-origin dual contributions**, so you have no mixing matrix to tune and no coordinator. That is CoCoA+'s "adding" rule, which was previously shown only for a star ([arXiv 1502.03508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.03508)), carried over to random gossip. Second, the Cho/Lai/Xu papers sum along a fixed tree or ring in synchronous rounds. You use a random k-out graph with randomized pushes. Third, almost all decentralized dual-coordinate methods need smooth losses ([arXiv 1810.02660](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02660)). You run the **true hinge loss and certify progress with a duality gap**. Fourth, the evaluation is in PeerSim with bytes as the headline metric, which no dual-method paper in the notes reports.

Expect three reviewer objections. The sharpest is **"this is CoCoA+ with an all-reduce done by epidemic broadcast."** The answer has to come from what flooding cannot give for free: behaviour under staleness and partial sums, where nodes hold different subsets of contributions, and a bytes-to-gap comparison against COLA. The second objection is that "add, don't average" looks like push-sum or gradient tracking, which also accumulate mass and then normalize. Cite subgradient-push (Nedić & Olshevsky) and Duchi's dual averaging and contrast with them directly. Note that the push-sum source itself was not checked in this session. The third objection is that CoCoA is a proximal ADMM in disguise ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00470)), which places your method in Forero's family. Your existing Forero baseline answers this empirically. A fourth point a careful reviewer will raise is memory and traffic: keeping state per origin costs up to N·d per node, and traffic grows with the number of contributions still in flight.

## Support-vector gossip already exists; the projection and dual-adding do not

The kernel literature falls into three groups. The **support-vector-exchange** group is the old threat. It includes Lu, Roychowdhury & Vandenberghe's DPSVM (IEEE TNN 2008), where nodes send local support vectors to neighbours and converge to the global optimum. It includes Xu, Wen & Wang's FDSVM (2012), which uses a "deterministic gossip protocol" to forward support vectors until every site holds the full set and converges in finite steps ([FDSVM page](https://journal.hep.com.cn/fee/EN/1159545315374195237)). It also includes the Flouri, Beferull-Lozano & Tsakalides SVMs for sensor networks: a sequential pass along a chain in 2006 ([EURASIP PDF](https://new.eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/Eusipco2006/papers/1568981880.pdf)), and selective-gossip versions MSG/SSG-SVM in 2008–2009 ([IEEE 7080490](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7080490)). Ang et al. propagate reduced support-vector sets in P2P networks with cascade RSVM ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-540-87479-9_22)). The **random-feature** group is the modern one. COKE/DKLA, QC-ODKLA, Bouboulis et al. and Richards et al. all send fixed-size random-feature weight vectors built from a common seed and solve with ADMM or diffusion ([COKE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10133)). The third group exchanges function values instead: Koppel et al. send raw points and get back scalar evaluations ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.04062)), and Forero's nonlinear version agrees on function values at shared reference points.

The DSRR paper, which your projection is built on, uses distribution only narrowly. It solves a projected **linear** problem with DisDCA in a master-worker setup, then uses that solution as a warm start for the full problem ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.03991)). It has no kernel, no gossip, and no final model that lives in the projected space. No paper found evaluates an RBF kernel on JL-projected inputs. Random Fourier features are a different construction: a projection followed by cos/sin ([arXiv 2108.04172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.04172)). The shared-seed trick itself is standard. Bajwa et al. regenerated projections from a common seed in 2006 ([PDF](https://nowak.ece.wisc.edu/ipsn06a.pdf)), and FedRP does the same today ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10041)). **Cite it as standard practice, not as a contribution.**

Two baselines matter most for the nonlinear method. ## The nonlinear method's novelty is narrower and must be stated precisely

**Closest prior art: FDSVM and DPSVM, then the Flouri gossip SVMs, then COKE/DKLA.** Do not claim to be the first decentralized SVM that exchanges support vectors. That claim is false. You can claim four things. First, the **SDCA local solver** with **signed duals that travel with each support vector, versioned per origin and added by receivers**. As far as the abstracts show, the older papers ship support-vector sets and **re-solve a QP on the union** at each node. Second, the **shared-seed DSRR projection applied to the support vectors themselves**, so messages shrink without ever sending the matrix. Third, an **RBF kernel evaluated in the projected space**, for which no paper was found. Fourth, **randomized asynchronous gossip** with bytes as the metric. Every fully decentralized kernel method found was synchronous or used deterministic flooding.

Reviewers will likely raise four objections. They will say the method is "FDSVM with compression". The answer is that FDSVM floods until every node has every support vector and re-solves, while you add duals incrementally. They will ask why you do not use random Fourier features like COKE. The answer is the message-size trade-off above, backed by a measured comparison. They will ask what the projection does to accuracy. A simple argument helps: the RBF kernel depends only on distances, and a JL projection preserves distances within a (1±ε) factor, so the kernel is preserved up to a bounded factor. The notes derived this argument themselves and did not find it in a published source, so you need a proof or a citation. Finally, reviewers will note the known flaw of exchanging only local support vectors: points that are not support vectors locally can become global support vectors, so the method can miss the global optimum ([search summary](http://users.ics.forth.gr/tsakalid/PAPERS/CNFRS/2009-DSP.pdf)). Your global duality gap should show whether your method suffers from this.

## What was actually read, and what to check before claiming novelty

The evidence is uneven. The researchers **read in full or in the method section**: gossip learning (Ormándi et al.), the first half of Lan & Zhou, the first two-thirds of DVR, Koppel et al., DSRR, and Flouri 2006. Everything about COLA's internals comes from abstracts plus memory. That covers synchronous rounds, primal averaging, the epfml/cola code and the hinge experiments. So do the Cho/Lai/Xu papers, COKE/DKLA, FDSVM, Ang et al. and ESDACD. DPSVM, the Flouri 2008/2009 gossip papers, the Management Science GADGET paper, Gbs-SVM, Kim et al. Semantic Scholar rate-limited the searches and Google Scholar was never queried, so all "not found" results mean not found, not proven absent.

## Conclusion

The two methods are novel in different ways, and the related-work sections should say so. The linear method's contribution is **a new way of combining**: adding per-origin dual contributions over random gossip, in place of COLA's averaging or a tree's fixed summation. Its risk is the "this is just broadcast" objection, so the experiments must show what happens under staleness and must report bytes to reach a given duality gap against COLA and P2Pegasos. The nonlinear method's contribution is **what goes in the message** (projected support vectors with signed duals that receivers add) rather than the idea of exchanging support vectors, which is about 15 years old. Its make-or-break evidence is the head-to-head against support-vector flooding and COKE on message size. The single most valuable next step is check 1: reading the three old support-vector-exchange papers in full, because they are the only works that could narrow either claim.
