# Nobody yet gossips, adds, or compresses SVM duals

**Bottom line: across nine sets of research notes, no published paper does either of your methods, and no paper compresses the messages of either one.** The two methods face different threats. The **linear method** (local SDCA, random k-out gossip, versioned per-origin A_k·Δα_k contributions that receivers add) has no direct prior art. Its nearest neighbours are COLA, which averages primal vectors with a mixing matrix instead of adding dual contributions, and the Cho/Lai/Xu tree and ring dual-coordinate-ascent papers, which are synchronous on a fixed topology. On compression, **only one paper, ACPD (Huo & Huang, arXiv 2019), compresses an SDCA/CoCoA+ message at all**. It top-k sparsifies the A_kΔα_k update, but it runs on a server, is only partially asynchronous, and uses ridge loss. No quantized CoCoA, COLA or SDCA paper turned up, and COLA's own authors list compression as future work. The **nonlinear method** has older prior art for its basic idea: sending support vectors around a network goes back to 2006–2012 (DPSVM, FDSVM, Flouri et al.). So "first decentralized SVM that exchanges support vectors" is false. What you can claim is the combination: an SDCA solver, signed duals that receivers add without re-solving, and support vectors shrunk by a shared-seed projection before the RBF kernel. Modern compressed decentralized kernel methods (COKE, QC-ODKLA, Ortega & Jafarkhani) do not send support vectors at all. They switch to a fixed-length random-feature vector and then censor or quantize changes to it. **None of them is asynchronous, and none of them sends compressed support vectors with their duals.** For your quantization ablation, three practical findings matter most. First, **CHOCO-SGD's own experiment grid (qsgd 4/8-bit, top-1%, rand-1%, plotted against bits) is almost identical to yours**, but its theory excludes the hinge loss. Second, top-k without error feedback will draw a reviewer request for an error-feedback variant. Third, the name "COLA" also belongs to a communication-censored ADMM method, so always cite He, Bian & Jaggi explicitly. For the nonlinear method's traffic problem, the most useful prior idea is old and simple: **MSG-SVM sends only support vectors not previously shared**. Because a projected support vector never changes, you can send each one once and afterwards send only (id, dual) changes. COKE-style censoring can then skip versions whose duals barely moved. One warning about the evidence: most of this was established from abstracts and snippets. The old support-vector-exchange papers, which matter most for the nonlinear claims, were still not read in full.

## The linear method's nearest rivals average where you add

The notes split the decentralized dual literature into two families, and the split protects the linear method. The first family uses the **consensus dual**: one dual variable per network edge, enforcing "my w equals your w". In these papers a gossip step turns out to be a coordinate step on that dual. Examples are ESDACD (AISTATS 2019) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02660)), Setwise Coordinate Descent (IEEE TAC 2025) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19004)), Notarnicola's randomized dual proximal gradient ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05713)) and StochaLM ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11432)). Almost all of them **require smooth, strongly convex losses**, so the hinge loss is outside their guarantees. The second family uses the **data dual** you use, with one α per training example and w built from those α's. Here the only decentralized member is COLA ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.04883)). Everything else in this family has a server or a cluster: Hybrid-DCA, Dis-dfSDCA, Nathan & Klabjan, and FedDCD ([arXiv 1610.10060](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.10060)). arXiv searches for "SDCA AND decentralized", "SDCA AND graph" and "CoCoA AND decentralized" **returned zero abstracts** ([arXiv API](https://export.arxiv.org/api/query?search_query=abs:SDCA+AND+abs:decentralized)).

A citation chase over the 144 Semantic Scholar citers of COLA and 179 citers of CoCoA+ found no gossip, asynchronous or additive extension of either ([S2 COLA citations](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/arXiv:1808.04883/citations)). What it found instead is the Cho/Lai/Xu line, which runs CoCoA-style dual coordinate ascent over a **tree** (JSAC 2021, ICASSP 2019) and a **ring** (Asilomar 2025). These are synchronous papers with fixed topologies and no random gossip ([arXiv 1703.04785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04785)). You already know the tree paper. The **Asilomar 2025 ring paper is new and should be cited**. It is the closest "CoCoA on a peer topology" work and was published recently. Newer work on decentralized SVMs has moved to primal methods on a smoothed hinge. Examples are Gbs-SVM, a 2026 SSRN preprint using gossip with gradient tracking ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6270798)), and the convoluted-SVM decentralized ADMM of 2025 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07563)).

## The linear method's novelty survives, framed as adding over gossip

**Closest prior art: COLA**, followed by the Cho/Lai/Xu tree and ring dual coordinate ascent. You can defend four differences. First, COLA mixes primal vectors with a doubly-stochastic matrix whose step size depends on the network's spectral gap. Your receivers instead **add versioned per-origin dual contributions**, so you have no mixing matrix to tune and no coordinator. That is CoCoA+'s "adding" rule, which was previously shown only for a star ([arXiv 1502.03508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.03508)), carried over to random gossip. Second, the Cho/Lai/Xu papers sum along a fixed tree or ring in synchronous rounds. You use a random k-out graph with randomized pushes. Third, almost all decentralized dual-coordinate methods need smooth losses ([arXiv 1810.02660](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02660)). You run the **true hinge loss and certify progress with a duality gap**. Fourth, the evaluation is in PeerSim with bytes as the headline metric, which no dual-method paper in the notes reports.

Expect three reviewer objections. The sharpest is **"this is CoCoA+ with an all-reduce done by epidemic broadcast."** The answer has to come from what flooding cannot give for free: behaviour under staleness and partial sums, where nodes hold different subsets of contributions, and a bytes-to-gap comparison against COLA. The second objection is that "add, don't average" looks like push-sum or gradient tracking, which also accumulate mass and then normalize. Cite subgradient-push (Nedić & Olshevsky) and Duchi's dual averaging and contrast with them directly. Note that the push-sum source itself was not checked in this session. The third objection is that CoCoA is a proximal ADMM in disguise ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00470)), which places your method in Forero's family. Your existing Forero baseline answers this empirically. A fourth point a careful reviewer will raise is memory and traffic: keeping state per origin costs up to N·d per node, and traffic grows with the number of contributions still in flight. The compression sections below are partly an answer to that fourth point.

## Only one paper compresses an SDCA message, and it uses a server

The compression literature for the linear setting is large on the primal side and almost empty on the dual side. On the dual side, targeted searches for "quantized CoCoA", "compressed SDCA", "COLA compression" and "error feedback dual coordinate ascent" found **exactly one paper that compresses the communicated update of a CoCoA+/SDCA-type method**: ACPD by Huo & Huang ([arXiv 1910.04235](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04235)). ACPD runs SDCA locally for H iterations, and each worker sends its primal-space update Δw_k = (1/λn)A_kΔα_k, which is **the same object as your per-origin contribution**. It keeps only the top-ρd entries by magnitude and sends them as location–value pairs ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.04235)). Its handling of the dropped part is the most instructive detail in the whole literature for you. In theory, ACPD pushes the dropped residual back into the dual variables, Δα̂ = λn A_k⁻¹(Δw_k∘¬M_k), so that w = (1/λn)Aα stays exactly true and the duality gap remains meaningful. In practice the matrix inverse is too expensive, so the authors simply keep the unsent residual and add it to the next round's message. That is plain **error feedback**, and the authors say it "does not affect the convergence empirically" without proving it ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.04235)). The setting is far from yours: a server, a group-wise partial asynchrony with staleness bounded by T−1, **ridge regression only, smooth-loss theory, and no bit or byte counts**. Cutting ρd from 10⁴ to 10 stayed stable above a gap of 10⁻⁴ and "degrades a little" below it ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.04235)).

Everything else in the CoCoA family saves communication by doing more local work per round, not by shrinking messages. CoCoA ([arXiv 1409.1458](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.1458)), CoCoA+ ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/mab15.pdf)), DisDCA ([PDF](https://people.tamu.edu/~tianbao-yang/papers/disdca.pdf)), the tree DCA papers ([arXiv 1703.04785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04785)), asynchronous dual-free SDCA ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.09066v2)) and FedDCD ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2201.11183)) all send full-precision vectors. COLA's conclusion lists "recent communication compression schemes" as **future work** ([arXiv 1808.04883](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04883)). No federated or server-based SVM paper that quantizes or sparsifies its transmitted vectors was found either ([PubMed, Fed-KSVM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37725743)).

The primal side is where the compressed-gossip toolkit lives, and **CHOCO-SGD is its canonical convex baseline**. CHOCO accepts any compressor that shrinks the error by a fixed fraction, including biased top-k, and it compensates with a "public copy" x̂ of each node's model that every neighbour also holds. Nodes send compressed *differences* from that copy ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00340)). Its convex experiments run L2-regularized logistic regression on epsilon and rcv1 with **qsgd at 2⁴ and 2⁸ levels, top-1% and rand-1%, measured in transmitted bits**, on ring, torus and complete graphs with 9 to 64 nodes ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00340)). That is nearly your ablation grid. Its theory assumes each local loss is **L-smooth and µ-strongly convex**, so hinge is outside it ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00340)). The closest *systems* relative is the Szeged gossip-learning line. Hegedűs, Danner & Jelasity ran asynchronous gossip learning in **PeerSim on a fixed random 20-out overlay** with logistic regression, compressing by random subsampling of 50%, 25% and 10% of the parameters, and they compared algorithms at equal bits per unit time ([DAIS 2019 PDF](https://www.inf.u-szeged.hu/~jelasity/cikkek/dais19a.pdf)). Their follow-up used stateful per-link codecs at about 2 bits per parameter, and observed that a larger out-degree makes each link talk less often, which hurts a codec that learns per link ([DICG 2020 PDF](https://www.inf.u-szeged.hu/~jelasity/cikkek/dicg20b.pdf)).

On hinge loss specifically, the picture is thin. None of the mainstream compressed-gossip methods (CHOCO, DCD/ECD, DeepSqueeze, Moniqua, LEAD, C-GT, CEDAS, BEER) has theory for a nonsmooth loss. Nonsmooth convex coverage under quantization comes only from quantized distributed **subgradient** methods, such as Doan, Maguluri & Romberg's adaptive quantization, whose experiments use quadratic and absolute loss ([arXiv 1810.13245](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.13245)). The only hinge-like quantized experiment found is a smoothed-hinge SVM case study inside a log-quantized primal consensus paper ([arXiv 2410.20345](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20345)). Asynchronous compressed methods do exist (SwarmSGD's random pairwise gossip with quantization ([NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/362c99307cdc3f2d8b410652386a9dd1-Abstract.html)), and the Szeged line), so "first asynchronous compressed gossip learner" is not claimable. Only the *dual* version is open.

## What this means for your quantization ablation

### Whole balances do not pile up rounding error; receipts do

The most important practical point is about what your messages contain. Think of a bank. If a branch reports its **full current balance** each time and the head office overwrites the old figure, a rounding error in one report is gone as soon as the next report arrives. If the branch instead sends **receipts for each transaction** and the head office adds them up, every rounded receipt leaves a small error in the running total, and those errors keep accumulating. ACPD sends receipts (incremental Δw_k) and therefore needs error feedback ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.04235)). If your versioning means a new version from origin j **replaces** the old one in each receiver's sum, you are sending balances. In that case the error a receiver holds about origin j is at most one compression error of the latest version, and it never grows. You need no residual memory, and the scheme tolerates lost or reordered messages. The notes found **no paper that analyses absolute versus incremental messages for any dual method** ([arXiv 1910.04235](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04235) is the only incremental one). That makes this a genuine positioning point, but it is the notes' own inference, not a published result, so it needs your own evidence: plot "versioned absolute + compressor" against "incremental + same compressor, no error feedback" and "incremental + error feedback". If your implementation actually sums increments, everything in this paragraph flips, and error feedback becomes mandatory.

### Top-10% is biased, and reviewers will ask for error feedback

QSGD is **unbiased**: on average it returns the true vector ([arXiv 1610.02132](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02132)). Top-k is **biased**: it always throws away the same small coordinates. In the primal literature, biased compressors are almost always paired with error feedback, through EF ([arXiv 1809.07599](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07599)), EF21 ([arXiv 2106.05203](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05203)) or CHOCO's public copies ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00340)). Karimireddy et al. show biased sign compression failing to reach the optimum on simple convex examples until error feedback is added ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/karimireddy19a.html)). With absolute messages, top-10% does not accumulate error, but it does leave a **permanent bias**: every receiver's sum misses the bottom 90% of every origin's contribution until that origin's next version reshuffles which coordinates are large. Expect that to show up as a duality-gap floor. Two remedies fit your design. One is an error-feedback arm (origin carries over what it did not send). The other is the CHOCO/COKE pattern of sending a compressed **difference from the last version the receiver already holds**. In random k-out gossip, different receivers hold different old versions, so a difference message must name its base version, and a receiver missing that base needs the full message. The DICG finding that per-link state works worse as out-degree grows applies directly here ([DICG 2020 PDF](https://www.inf.u-szeged.hu/~jelasity/cikkek/dicg20b.pdf)). That design is the notes' inference and has no published precedent.

### Lossless sparse and QSGD need careful bit accounting

Your lossless index+value arm matches what Stich et al. observe on RCV1: when the data is sparse, plain SGD already exploits gradient sparsity, so top-k gains less ([arXiv 1809.07599](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07599)). An SDCA contribution A_kΔα_k is a weighted sum of the rows the local pass touched, so it inherits the data's sparsity. With 4-byte indices and 8-byte values, index+value costs 12 bytes per nonzero against 8 per coordinate dense, so it pays only below about two-thirds density (simple arithmetic, not from the notes). QSGD's published form includes **Elias integer coding** of the levels, and Stich et al. note bit counts "can be reduced using Elias coding" ([arXiv 1809.07599](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07599)). State whether your 8-bit and 4-bit QSGD counts fixed-width bits or entropy-coded bits, and whether the norm and sign bits are included. Also state how your bit-width maps to CHOCO's qsgd parameter, which is given as a number of levels s ∈ {2⁴, 2⁸} rather than bits per coordinate ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00340)). Reporting **suboptimality or duality gap against transmitted bits** matches CHOCO's metric and makes the figures legible to reviewers. ACPD never reported bits, so a bits-to-gap curve for a dual method would be the first of its kind in this literature ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.04235)).

### The duality-gap certificate still works if you evaluate it honestly

Compression breaks the exact link w = (1/λn)Aα at receivers, since the w a node uses is built from compressed contributions. ACPD protected the certificate by changing α to match what was sent ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.04235)). A simpler route follows from weak duality: any w gives a primal value at least as large as any dual value, so **P(ŵ) − D(α), evaluated at the decompressed ŵ a node actually holds, is still a valid upper bound** on how suboptimal ŵ is. The notes raise this route but found no paper that uses it for hinge-loss SDCA under quantization. Report the gap this way and say so.

### Per-baseline caveats

**CHOCO-SGD on hinge loss runs outside its theory.** It works with subgradients, but its Assumption 2 requires smooth, strongly convex losses ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00340)). Say this in one sentence, and keep CHOCO's own error compensation switched on, since that is part of the published method. **COLA plus QSGD or top-k is your own extension.** No paper compresses COLA, and its authors list compression as future work ([arXiv 1808.04883](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04883)). Apply each compressor to COLA's exchanged vectors exactly as you apply it to yours, and label the arm "COLA + compressor (our extension)". **The name clash needs one sentence.** "COLA" also names Communication-Censored Linearized ADMM by Li, Liu, Tian & Ling (IEEE TSIPN 2020), the sibling of COCA ([IEEE SPS](https://signalprocessingsociety.org/publications-resources/ieee-transactions-signal-processing/2019/05/communication-censored-admm-decentralized-consensus-optimization)). A reviewer searching "COLA compression" will find the censored one, so cite "COLA (He, Bian & Jaggi, NeurIPS 2018)" every time, or add a footnote. **GADGET plus compression is also your own extension.** No compressed Pegasos-gossip paper was found. The closest published analogue is the Szeged subsampling scheme on gossip learning, which you could add as a rand-10% arm next to top-10%, with rescaling for unbiasedness ([DAIS 2019 PDF](https://www.inf.u-szeged.hu/~jelasity/cikkek/dais19a.pdf)). That arm costs little and directly separates "biased selection" from "random selection" at the same budget.

## Support-vector gossip already exists; the projection and dual-adding do not

The kernel literature falls into three groups. The **support-vector-exchange** group is the old threat. It includes Lu, Roychowdhury & Vandenberghe's DPSVM (IEEE TNN 2008), where nodes send local support vectors to neighbours and converge to the global optimum. It includes Xu, Wen & Wang's FDSVM (2012), which uses a "deterministic gossip protocol" to forward support vectors until every site holds the full set and converges in finite steps ([FDSVM page](https://journal.hep.com.cn/fee/EN/1159545315374195237)). It also includes the Flouri, Beferull-Lozano & Tsakalides SVMs for sensor networks: a sequential pass along a chain in 2006 ([EURASIP PDF](https://new.eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/Eusipco2006/papers/1568981880.pdf)), and selective-gossip versions MSG/SSG-SVM in 2008–2009 ([IEEE 7080490](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7080490)). Ang et al. propagate reduced support-vector sets in P2P networks with cascade RSVM ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-540-87479-9_22)). The **random-feature** group is the modern one. COKE/DKLA, QC-ODKLA, Bouboulis et al. and Richards et al. all send fixed-size random-feature weight vectors and solve with ADMM or diffusion. COKE states that the random features must be drawn identically at every agent, which is the same idea as your shared seed ([COKE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10133)). The third group exchanges function values instead: Koppel et al. send raw points and get back scalar evaluations ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.04062)), and Forero's nonlinear version agrees on function values at shared reference points ([JMLR PDF](https://jmlr2020.csail.mit.edu/papers/volume11/forero10a/forero10a.pdf)).

The DSRR paper, which your projection is built on, uses distribution only narrowly. It solves a projected **linear** problem with DisDCA in a master-worker setup, then uses that solution as a warm start for the full problem ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.03991)). It has no kernel, no gossip, and no final model that lives in the projected space. No paper found evaluates an RBF kernel on JL-projected inputs, in either the earlier or the new round of searches. Random Fourier features are a different construction: a projection followed by cos/sin ([arXiv 2108.04172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.04172)). The closest relatives are Mangasarian & Wild's privacy-preserving SVM with a random reduced kernel, which needs a coordinator ([UW 07-03](https://ftp1.cs.wisc.edu/pub/dmi/tech-reports/07-03.pdf)), and the general result that a JL projection before a linear SVM preserves margins ([arXiv 1911.10800](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.10800)). The shared-seed trick itself is standard. Bajwa et al. regenerated projections from a common seed in 2006 ([PDF](https://nowak.ece.wisc.edu/ipsn06a.pdf)), FedRP does the same today ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10041)), and FDSKL regenerates random directions from a shared seed so features never travel ([arXiv 2008.06197](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06197)). **Cite it as standard practice, not as a contribution.** One difference is worth stating: in the RFF methods, the shared randomness fixes the *model's* size, while in yours it shrinks the *payload*, the support vectors themselves, from d to m dimensions.

## The nonlinear method's novelty is narrower and must be stated precisely

**Closest prior art: FDSVM and DPSVM, then the Flouri gossip SVMs, then COKE/DKLA.** Do not claim to be the first decentralized SVM that exchanges support vectors. That claim is false. You can claim four things. First, the **SDCA local solver** with **signed duals that travel with each support vector, versioned per origin and added by receivers**. As far as the abstracts show, the older papers ship support-vector sets and **re-solve a QP on the union** at each node. Second, the **shared-seed DSRR projection applied to the support vectors themselves**, so messages shrink without ever sending the matrix. Third, an **RBF kernel evaluated in the projected space**, for which no paper was found. Fourth, **randomized asynchronous gossip** with bytes as the metric. Every fully decentralized kernel method found was synchronous or used deterministic flooding, and the new compression search found no asynchronous compressed decentralized kernel method either.

Reviewers will likely raise five objections. They will say the method is "FDSVM with compression". The answer is that FDSVM floods until every node has every support vector and re-solves, while you add duals incrementally. They will ask why you do not use random Fourier features like COKE. The answer is the message-size trade-off above, backed by a measured comparison. They will quote Bouboulis et al. back at you: without random features, a kernel diffusion method "would require from each node to transmit its entire growing sum (i.e., the coefficients a_i as well as the respective centers x_i)", so communication grows over time ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.08131)). Your current design, which re-sends full support-vector blocks per version, is exactly what that sentence describes, so the fix in the next section matters for the paper, not just for performance. They will ask what the projection does to accuracy. A simple argument helps: the RBF kernel depends only on distances, and a JL projection preserves distances within a (1±ε) factor, so the kernel is preserved up to a bounded factor. The notes derived this argument themselves and did not find it in a published source, so you need a proof or a citation. Finally, reviewers will note the known flaw of exchanging only local support vectors: points that are not support vectors locally can become global support vectors, so the method can miss the global optimum ([DSP 2009 PDF](http://users.ics.forth.gr/tsakalid/PAPERS/CNFRS/2009-DSP.pdf)). The survey description of MSG-SVM confirms that this selective scheme is "efficient but suboptimal", while the convex-hull variant SSG-SVM reaches the optimum at about three times the traffic ([survey arXiv 1606.03508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.03508)). Your global duality gap should show whether your method suffers from this.

## Kernel methods compress by switching to random features, then censoring

The modern compressed decentralized kernel literature nearly always follows one recipe: convert the model into a fixed-length random-feature vector, then **censor** or **quantize** changes to that vector. COKE motivates the switch directly: kernel decision variables "are data-dependent and thus cannot be optimized under the decentralized consensus framework without any raw data exchange" ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10133)). Its censoring rule is like a colleague who only phones in when something has actually changed. Agent i sends its weights θ_i only if they have moved far enough from the last version it sent, ‖θ_i^k − θ̂_i^{k−1}‖ ≥ v·µ^k, with a threshold that shrinks over time (µ = 0.95–0.98 in the experiments). The result was **"almost 50%" fewer transmissions** for a negligible accuracy loss on squared-loss Gaussian-kernel regression ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10133)). QC-ODKLA by the same group adds quantization: it sends the **difference from the last broadcast state** with a uniform quantizer at **3 bits per element** (against 32 for its baselines), censors with threshold α·β^t, and reports MSE against total bits on six UCI regression sets ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.02777)). Ortega & Jafarkhani do the same over gossip, quantizing the difference to a CHOCO-style shared hidden state at 3 bits per element, for kernel logistic regression on Banana, Credit-Card and MNIST ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09848v2)). All of these are synchronous, and none uses hinge loss.

The older family that keeps an expansion over data points compresses differently. MSG-SVM sends **only support vectors not previously shared**, cutting traffic about threefold relative to SSG-SVM ([survey arXiv 1606.03508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.03508)). In kernel adaptive filtering, Mitra & Bhatia's QDKLMS vector-quantizes the distributed dictionary by merging nearby centres, and their FBDKLMS prunes to a fixed budget ([arXiv 1509.02730](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02730)). Koppel et al. compress the local dictionary with KOMP, but that limits memory, not messages, since they send a raw sample and receive scalars ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.04062)). Forero's MoM-NDSVM sidesteps the issue with a **fixed (L+1)-float message** per neighbour per iteration, regardless of how many support vectors exist, and claims robustness to quantization noise ([JMLR PDF](https://jmlr2020.csail.mit.edu/papers/volume11/forero10a/forero10a.pdf)). Searches for quantized or projected support-vector exchange in sensor-network SVMs returned only selection and redundancy-removal methods ([EUSIPCO 2006 PDF](https://new.eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/Eusipco2006/papers/1568981880.pdf)). **No paper was found that sends compressed, quantized or projected support vectors together with their dual coefficients over a decentralized graph**, after about 25 searches and fetches.

## Send each support vector once, then send only dual changes

Your nonlinear method's traffic problem has a cheap fix hiding in its own structure, and MSG-SVM is the precedent to cite for it. The projection is fixed by the shared seed, so **a training point's projected vector never changes**. Between versions, what changes is which points are support vectors and what their signed duals are. Re-sending the full block per version is like re-mailing the whole phone book every time one number changes. Instead, an origin can send a projected vector the first time a receiver needs it, and afterwards send only (point id, new dual) pairs for duals that changed, plus ids of points that dropped out. With m = 512 and float64, one projected vector is 4,096 bytes, while an id plus a dual is about 12–16 bytes. So every unchanged support vector you stop re-sending saves roughly a 250-fold factor on that item (this is arithmetic on your stated settings, not a published result). Unlike MSG-SVM's selection, this is **lossless**: it changes only what travels, not what the receiver ends up holding, so it does not inherit MSG's suboptimality ([survey arXiv 1606.03508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.03508)). In gossip, the origin cannot know exactly what each random peer already holds, so the message must name a base version, and a receiver missing it falls back to the full block. That is the same issue as delta messages in the linear method.

Three further ideas from the random-feature line transfer cleanly once diffs are in place. **Censoring** (COKE, QC-ODKLA): skip publishing a new version when the dual change since the last published one is below a threshold that decays over time, which cut transmissions by about half in COKE ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10133)). A decaying threshold matters, because a fixed one would stall convergence near the end. **Quantized deltas** (QC-ODKLA, Ortega): quantize the dual changes or the projected vectors, against a last-sent reference, at a few bits per element ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.02777)). After the diff fix, though, vectors travel once and duals are already small, so quantization buys much less than it does in the random-feature methods. If you quantize the projected vectors, remember that the RBF kernel depends on distances, so quantization noise stacks on top of the JL distortion. Keep the duals exact, since they enforce the box constraint and drive the gap certificate. **Centre merging** (QDKLMS): merge nearby support vectors into one with a combined weight, which bounds message size but changes the model ([arXiv 1509.02730](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02730)). It is the riskiest of the three and probably belongs in future work. For reporting, Forero's fixed (L+1) floats per neighbour per iteration ([JMLR PDF](https://jmlr2020.csail.mit.edu/papers/volume11/forero10a/forero10a.pdf)) and COKE's L-float RF vector are the natural yardsticks for bytes per round.

## What survives once compression is added

For the **linear method**, the claim "first compressed decentralized gossip dual coordinate ascent" survives, qualified with "to our knowledge". ACPD is the only compressed SDCA/CoCoA+ method and it is server-based, partially synchronous and ridge-only ([arXiv 1910.04235](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04235)). Also apparently unpublished are quantized (low-bit) CoCoA, COLA or SDCA in any topology, any hinge-loss experiment with compression in the CoCoA/SDCA family, and any bits-to-duality-gap curve for a dual method. The **versioned-absolute message as a way to avoid error accumulation without residual memory** is a defensible design contribution, but only with your own ablation behind it, since no paper discusses absolute versus incremental dual messages. Claims that **do not** survive: "first asynchronous compressed gossip learning" (SwarmSGD; Hegedűs et al.), "first compressed decentralized SVM" in a loose sense (the log-quantized smoothed-hinge case study, [arXiv 2410.20345](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20345)), and anything implying CHOCO-style error compensation is new.

For the **nonlinear method**, the claim "first to send compressed (projected) support vectors with their dual coefficients over a decentralized graph" survives as absence of evidence. QDKLMS's centre quantization in kernel adaptive filtering is the nearest thing ([arXiv 1509.02730](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02730)), and Bouboulis et al. said in 2017 that dictionary-sharing strategies had "not appeared in the respective literature" ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.08131)). "First asynchronous compressed decentralized kernel method" also survives, because every compressed kernel method found is synchronous. Two cautions apply. Delta-style transmission and decaying-threshold censoring are COKE/QC-ODKLA's ideas, and "send only new SVs" is MSG-SVM's, so if you adopt them, credit them rather than claiming them. And the full texts of Flouri 2008/2009, DPSVM and FDSVM were still not readable, so it remains unknown whether any of them sends α's with the support vectors.

## Conclusion

Compression changes the shape of both stories. For the linear method, the interesting question is no longer "does SDCA tolerate 4-bit messages?", which CHOCO and Stich et al. have effectively answered for primal methods. It is **whether sending whole per-origin balances instead of receipts removes the need for error feedback under gossip**. That is a question only a dual, additive, versioned design can even ask, and no paper has asked it. An ablation that puts versioned-absolute, incremental-without-EF and incremental-with-EF side by side, with gap measured at the decompressed point and plotted against bits, would turn a routine compression table into a result. For the nonlinear method, the real compression win is not a quantizer but bookkeeping. Projected support vectors are immutable, so diffs plus COKE-style censoring should shrink traffic far more than any bit-width change, and that shrinkage is also the direct rebuttal to the "growing sums" critique that motivated the whole random-feature literature. Measure traffic against COKE's fixed-size vector after that fix, not before it. Otherwise you will be comparing against a version of your own method that a reviewer can wave away in one sentence.
