# Your baselines never left one machine either

**All six of your baselines were originally evaluated on a single computer. None ran on separate machines over a real network.** GADGET and Forero's MoM-NDSVM were simulations (PeerSim and MATLAB). COLA ran one thread per node on one 24-core server. CHOCO-SGD's convex experiments describe no hardware and plot results against iterations and bits. Fed-KSVM, the open item, is settled by the paper itself: its edge devices were "simulated by M worker processes and one coordinator process" talking over MPI in lockstep, and its "memory constraint" was imitated by changing a block-count parameter rather than measured on any device. Real machine-to-machine deployments of gossip or decentralized first-order optimization do exist, but nearly all of them train deep networks on GPU clusters (D-PSGD, AD-PSGD, SGP, GossipGraD, SwarmSGD, MATCHA, Moniqua) or small neural networks on 4 to 10 edge boards (NEBULA, Gist, Yu et al.). For convex or SVM workloads, the verified truly decentralized real runs come down to three papers: Tsianos et al.'s consensus dual averaging on 14 Ethernet nodes, Ang et al.'s P2P kernel SVM (500 logical peers on 16 machines), and Hop's webspam SVM on 4 machines. **I found no paper that ran a gossip or decentralized SDCA/dual-coordinate method on physically separate machines.** Every real SDCA deployment (the CoCoA family, MPI-LIBLINEAR, SnapML) uses a driver or all-reduce. This makes your PeerSim-style evaluation the same protocol your baselines used, so the comparison is fair. It also means a real-network demo of asynchronous gossip SDCA would fill an open gap rather than repeat known work.

A useful way to keep the categories straight is to compare a rehearsal with a touring show. A single-machine simulation is a rehearsal where every actor is in the same room: messages arrive instantly or after a delay the director chose, and nobody's phone rings. A real deployment is the tour, with late trains, sick actors and bad sound systems. Most of the papers below rehearsed. The few that toured mostly found that gossip helps when the travel is bad.

COLA is the instructive case. It used PyTorch with an MPI backend, which sounds like a cluster, yet the paper states that the topology "is simulated by running one thread per graph node, on a 2×12 core Intel Xeon CPU E5-2680 v3 server" ([arXiv 1808.04883](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04883)). Using an MPI library therefore does not show that a paper used several machines, and the same caution applies to Fed-KSVM.

### Fed-KSVM simulated its edge devices and their memory limits

I read Section VI-A of the local copy (`notes/research_papers/A_Memory-Efficient_Federated_Kernel_Support_Vector_Machine_for_Edge_Devices.pdf`) directly. The key sentence is: **"The system of M edge devices and one central server is simulated by M worker processes and one coordinator process. The worker processes communicate with the coordinator process via the message-passing interface (MPI) in a synchronized manner"** ([Zhou & Wang, TNNLS 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnnls.2023.3302802)). The paper does not name a CPU, a machine count, a network or a single physical edge device. Three other details point the same way. The paper says the number of devices M "increases as a dataset with a larger size is used in the simulation". The memory constraint is not measured either: "the value of L is selected from {1, 2, 4, 5, 10} to simulate different settings of the memory constraint". Communication cost is "measured by the volume of the model parameters exchanged", which is a count and not a network measurement. Each scheme ran **20 communication rounds, repeated ten times**, on six datasets (UWB, IJCNN1, Skin-nonskin, UNSW-NB15, Webspam, KDDCup99). The headline results, **up to 99% less communication than centralized training and about 90% less device memory**, are bookkeeping results from that simulation ([Zhou & Wang, TNNLS 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnnls.2023.3302802)).

Two details from the paper matter for your positioning. Fed-KSVM's local solver is SDCA (its hyperparameter H is "the number of iterations of SDCA to optimize each block"), and one of its baselines is random features plus CoCoA. So Fed-KSVM is a member of the server-based CoCoA/SDCA family, and your comparison with it is SDCA against SDCA, with the server replaced by gossip. Because Fed-KSVM's processes are synchronous and its "memory on edge devices" figures are counted rather than measured, your simulator can reproduce its setting faithfully without hardware.

Three things stand out. First, **Tsianos et al. is the only verified truly decentralized convex optimizer on separate physical machines**, and it is a 2012 result on Pentium 4 nodes. It is still a useful precedent. The authors report "strong agreement" between their theory and the cluster, and they found that communicating less often over time improves speed ([arXiv 1209.1076](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.1076)). Second, Ang et al. is the closest precedent for your kernel method, a P2P RBF SVM, but its 500 peers were packed onto 16 machines in an unexplained way, so it is part real and part emulated ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-540-87479-9_22.pdf)). Third, Hop is the only real-cluster decentralized run with an SVM-type workload, and it swapped hinge loss for log loss and injected its own stragglers: "We simulate a heterogeneous environment by randomly slowing down every worker by 6 times" ([arXiv 1902.01064](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.01064)).

The deep-learning cluster papers agree on one lesson. **Gossip beats a central server or all-reduce only when the network or some workers are slow.** D-PSGD says that with high bandwidth and low latency "all three SGD implementations have similar speed" ([arXiv 1705.09056](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09056)). SGP found that on InfiniBand "all methods exhibit a near linear scaling" ([arXiv 1811.10792](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10792)). The analogy is road traffic: side streets beat a single highway only when the highway is jammed. Many of these papers also built the jam themselves, using Linux `tc` throttling (D-PSGD, Moniqua) or slowdowns they injected (Hop). So even the real deployments contain simulated parts.

The edge testbeds are honest about their size. Physical setups top out at **about 10 devices**, and every scale claim moves to emulation or simulation: Gist goes to 200 devices only in QEMU ([Gist PDF](https://asadi.io/files/papers/NavidrezaAsadi_MobiCom2025-FLEdgeAI_Gist.pdf)). None trains an SVM or uses a dual or coordinate method. I found no verified real-hardware gossip or consensus learning on robot swarms, vehicles or sensor motes. The robot-swarm FL paper used the ARGoS simulator ([arXiv 2409.01900](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.01900)), and the wireless-sensor consensus SVM was simulation-only ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7080490)). Real motes have run consensus, but for clock synchronization, not learning ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315830285_A_Distributed_Consensus-Based_Clock_Synchronization_Protocol_for_Wireless_Sensor_Networks)).

Federated learning has the largest real deployments of all, and all of them have a server. Google's system ran synchronous FedAvg on Android phones that were idle, charging and on unmetered Wi-Fi, with "approximately 10M daily active devices" and a few hundred clients per round. Training was about **7× slower than a datacenter**, and 6–10% of devices dropped out each round ([arXiv 1902.01046](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.01046)). The Gboard model was tested "in live production experiments" ([arXiv 1811.03604](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.03604)). Meta's Papaya ran asynchronous and synchronous FL side by side on nearly 100 million Android phones and found **asynchronous FL 5× faster with nearly 8× less communication** at high concurrency ([arXiv 2111.04877](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.04877)). That is the strongest real-world evidence that asynchrony pays off, though it comes from a server-based system. Lab testbeds are smaller and also synchronous: Flower ran Jetson, Raspberry Pi and Android clients against a cloud-VM FedAvg server ([arXiv 2007.14390](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.14390v5)), and COLEXT used 28 single-board computers plus 20 phones over Ethernet and Wi-Fi ([arXiv 2407.14154](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.14154v2)). None of these trained an SVM or ran SDCA.

A middle group calls itself "decentralized" but still averages exactly within groups. Prague uses Partial All-Reduce ([ASPLOS'20](https://asplos.hosting2.acm.org/wp/wp-content/uploads/2020/abstracts/paper_4_4.html)). Moshpit finds peers through a DHT and runs all-reduce in small groups, on 64 mixed servers over 1 Gb/s Ethernet ([arXiv 2103.03239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03239)). DeDLOC trained with real internet volunteers and can mimic a parameter server, all-reduce or gossip ([arXiv 2106.10207](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10207)). SWARM ran on 400 preemptible T4 instances ([arXiv 2301.11913](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11913)). INTELLECT-1 spanned 3 continents with a global all-reduce roughly every 38 minutes ([arXiv 2412.01152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01152)). In these LLM-era papers "decentralized" means no single owner of the hardware, not gossip averaging. They are real deployments but not peer-to-peer in your sense.

## Famous decentralized and federated papers that were simulations

Many papers that read as deployments were rehearsals. Among gossip learning papers, Ormándi, Hegedűs and Jelasity's P2Pegasos ran in "the PeerSim event based P2P simulator" ([arXiv 1109.1396](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.1396)). Hegedűs, Danner and Jelasity's gossip-versus-federated comparison also used PeerSim, with smartphone churn traces from 1,191 real users ([DAIS 2019](http://www.inf.u-szeged.hu/~ihegedus/publ/dais19a.pdf)). Bao et al.'s TinyML bilayer gossip SGD ran "sequentially on a single computer" and lists "the lack of real-world testing instances on edge devices" as a limitation ([arXiv 2501.04817](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.04817v1)). In optimization, PASSCoDe's asynchronous dual coordinate descent runs on one shared-memory machine ([arXiv 1504.01365](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.01365)), and D² describes no hardware ([arXiv 1803.07068](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07068)). The federated algorithms used most often as baselines are simulations too. FedAvg partitioned MNIST into 100 virtual clients ([arXiv 1602.05629](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.05629)). FedProx states, "We simulate systems heterogeneity" and reports rounds, not wall-clock time ([arXiv 1812.06127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.06127)). FedBuff "built a simulator", with delays fitted to Meta's production traces ([arXiv 2106.06639](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.06639v4)). FedScale is mainly trace-driven simulation ([arXiv 2105.11367](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.11367)). COLEXT puts it bluntly: "Despite its research popularity, FL remains mostly confined to simulations" ([arXiv 2407.14154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14154)).

Several real deployments also have simulated parts. Plexus ran on real cluster GPUs but states, "we simulate the passing of time" ([arXiv 2302.13837](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13837)). Yuan et al. used 64 real V100s with "geo-distributed devices simulated using real-world network measurements" ([arXiv 2206.01288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01288)). Flower's 15-million-client result ran on two GPUs ([arXiv 2007.14390](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14390)). "Real" and "simulated" are better treated as a range than as two boxes. The honest test is whether network delays and failures were measured or chosen by the authors.

## What this means for your asynchronous gossip SDCA

**Your evaluation protocol matches your baselines' original protocols, so the comparison is fair.** Every decentralized baseline (GADGET, COLA, CHOCO-SGD convex, MoM-NDSVM) and the federated one (Fed-KSVM) was evaluated on one machine with synthetic or no timing. One sentence you can defend in a paper: "All decentralized SVM and linear-model baselines, and Fed-KSVM, were originally evaluated in single-machine simulation; real-network deployments in the SDCA family are coordinator- or all-reduce-based." Your simulator also reproduces Fed-KSVM's setting more naturally than it reproduces a real network. Fed-KSVM's devices were synchronous MPI processes and its memory limit was a parameter, so a simulated Fed-KSVM loses nothing that the original paper had.

The limits matter as much. A simulator cannot back claims about wall-clock speed or energy, which are the claims real deployments exist to test. The real-cluster papers show that gossip's advantage depends on the network: it appears on slow or uneven links and disappears on fast ones ([arXiv 1705.09056](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09056), [arXiv 1811.10792](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10792)). It is also where asynchrony pays most. AD-PSGD's largest wins came with heterogeneous workers ([arXiv 1710.06952](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06952)), and Papaya's 5× came from a real population of devices with varied speeds ([arXiv 2111.04877](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.04877)). So results in a simulator should be reported in measures that do not need real time, such as rounds, messages and bytes, which is what CHOCO-SGD and Fed-KSVM did. Any time axis should state the delay model it assumes. The strongest step short of hardware is the Hegedűs et al. practice of driving churn and availability from real device traces ([DAIS 2019](http://www.inf.u-szeged.hu/~ihegedus/publ/dais19a.pdf)).

There is also an opening. Nobody has toured a decentralized SDCA. The nearest work is Tsianos et al. (dual averaging, not SDCA, synchronous, 2012) and Ang et al. (a kernel SVM by cascade, not SDCA, with emulated peers). A small physical demo would therefore be new in its own right. The edge papers show the expected scale: NEBULA used 4 boards on wired Ethernet and Gist used 10 microcontrollers ([arXiv 2505.08033](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08033), [Gist PDF](https://asadi.io/files/papers/NavidrezaAsadi_MobiCom2025-FLEdgeAI_Gist.pdf)). A demo of that size, for example 5 to 10 Raspberry Pis or LAN machines exchanging SDCA updates over TCP, alongside the simulator for scale, follows the same "small real demo plus large simulation" pattern that Gist and Epidemic Learning were published with.

Some points remain uncertain. CHOCO-SGD ICML 2019 is classified "simulated" because no hardware sentence was found, not because the paper says it was simulated. Its code repository was not checked. DisDCA does not state its machine count. Ang et al. do not explain how 500 peers mapped onto 16 machines. The search was broad but not exhaustive. Spark-LIBLINEAR, DSVM-AVE, the full text of Wulfert & Grabmaier's microcontroller gossip paper, and the Flouri et al. sensor-network SVMs were not checked. One of these could still turn out to be a real decentralized SVM run.

## Conclusion

The rehearsal-versus-tour split runs differently from what the field's vocabulary suggests. "Decentralized" and "edge" in a title say little about whether separate machines were used. The algorithm papers that people compare against, on the decentralized and the federated side, were mostly simulated. The papers that toured were systems papers whose goal was to measure the network. So the choice that matters for your work is which claims you make, not whether you simulate. Claims about convergence per round, communication volume, robustness to dropped messages and tolerance of stale updates are fully supported by a PeerSim-style simulator and compare like for like with all six baselines. Claims about seconds or joules need at least a small testbed.

The open niche is narrow and real. In SDCA, the work that runs over real networks is all coordinator-based, and the work that is decentralized was all simulated. An asynchronous gossip SDCA that runs over a real network, even on a handful of boards, would be the first paper to combine the two, and it would test the regime where the evidence says gossip and asynchrony help: slow, uneven, unreliable links.
